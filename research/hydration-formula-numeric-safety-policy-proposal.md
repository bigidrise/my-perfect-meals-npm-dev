# My Perfect Meals Hydration Formula & Numeric Safety Policy — research proposal

## Policy objective and scope

This is a population-level product safety policy, not an individualized medical prescription. It applies to exercise, heat, sweat loss, altitude, and occupational heat contexts. Environmental labels (hot, altitude, long duration, work) are risk/context signals; they are **not** validated inputs for automatic individualized fluid or sodium multipliers.

## Formula policy

1. **Default: do not produce a personalized fluid-volume number** for an exercise, heat, sweat-loss, altitude, or occupational-heat scenario solely from profile data, planned duration/intensity, weather, elevation, or a self-reported “heavy sweater” label.
2. **No automatic context adjustments.** Heat, acclimatization, clothing/PPE, intensity, and body size materially affect sweat rate; NATA directs assessment multiple times in realistic conditions. Altitude is named as a setting with hydration nuances, but the reviewed evidence supplies no universal altitude volume adjustment. Therefore none of those variables may automatically increase a person’s numeric target.
3. **Measured-sweat-rate pathway only.** A numeric *planning range* may be considered only when a recent, repeatable, same-person sweat-loss assessment under substantially comparable activity/environment is available, including pre/post body mass, exercise duration, fluid consumed, and urine volume. The source equation is: sweat loss = pre-exercise mass − post-exercise mass + fluid consumed − urine; divide by duration for sweat rate. Display the provenance, conditions, date, uncertainty, and that this is not a medical prescription.
4. **Hard safety guardrails for a measured pathway.** Do not recommend intake exceeding observed exercise-related body-mass losses; do not present a plan that anticipates body-mass gain during activity. NATA’s performance-oriented target is to limit exercise body-mass loss to below 2%, and its cited euhydration band is +1% to −1%; these are population-level athletic-management reference bounds, not universal user targets or a license to calculate an unmeasured volume.
5. **Post-exercise numbers are conditional.** The 100%–150% fluid-loss replacement range is only a recovery reference when loss is measured and recovery is short (<4 h). It must not be displayed as a generic post-workout amount.
6. **Thirst/ad-libitum fallback.** For generally healthy, active people who do not know sweat rate, NATA says drinking to thirst is likely safe for preventing overdrinking; the EAH consensus recommends drinking to thirst to reduce EAH risk. This should be a nonnumeric general-education message, not an assurance of performance optimization. It does not override employer heat controls: OSHA says workers should drink frequently in heat, not only when thirsty.
7. **Electrolyte/sodium policy.** Never infer an individual sodium dose, concentration, or tablet recommendation from exercise duration, heat, altitude, sweat volume estimate, cramps, or “heavy sweater” status. NATA ties possible individual supplementation to measured high sweat sodium (>60 mEq/L) together with high sweat rate (>2.5 L/h) and says specific supplementation is not needed for activity under one hour; it also notes limited evidence for sodium tablets during activity. OSHA’s “>2 hours provide electrolyte-containing beverages” is an employer availability rule, not a personal sodium prescription. Sodium in a sports drink cannot make overdrinking safe: the EAH consensus says hypotonic sports drinks remain vulnerable to dilution from excessive volume.

## Mandatory number-withholding conditions

Withhold a numeric hydration or electrolyte recommendation, show only safety education and escalation language, when:

- sweat loss/rate has not been measured repeatedly in comparable real conditions;
- the request depends on a heat, humidity, altitude, PPE/clothing, acclimatization, duration, or occupational-work adjustment alone;
- the product would need to estimate sodium concentration, sodium dose, or electrolyte concentration without measured sweat sodium and a qualified plan;
- planned intake could exceed observed losses or imply exercise body-mass gain;
- symptoms or signs compatible with EAH/serious illness are reported (including progressive headache, swelling, confusion, vomiting, breathing difficulty, collapse, or seizure), or acute heat illness is suspected — no self-treatment number; seek urgent onsite/emergency evaluation according to severity;
- the person reports a condition, medication, or clinician-directed fluid/sodium restriction that can alter water/electrolyte handling, or is requesting a therapeutic correction plan;
- a workplace scenario lacks an employer heat plan, water/rest/shade access, supervisory controls, or required risk assessment. Direct to employer/occupational-health procedures rather than calculate a worker dose.

## Required product wording and controls

- Label every output “population-level planning reference, not personal medical advice.”
- Distinguish **reference bound**, **measured-data calculation**, **general thirst guidance**, and **employer-provided beverage availability**; never blend them into one target.
- Require a reasoned comparison check before reusing a sweat-rate record (sport/task, duration, intensity, weather/heat, altitude, clothing/PPE, acclimatization).
- Display EAH/overdrinking warning with every measured-rate screen: body-mass gain during activity is a warning signal; excess hypotonic fluid is the primary preventable exposure in the consensus evidence.
- Never market electrolyte drinks/tablets as preventing EAH or heat illness.

## Evidence dates and population scope

The NATA and EAH sources are athletic/physically active population guidance and consensus, respectively; they are not prescriptions for all ages, health states, workplaces, or altitude exposures. OSHA is employer/workplace prevention guidance, not individualized clinical dosing. The ACSM, CDC/NIOSH, altitude-study, and IAAF search-only citations require full-text/date verification before being used to add a product rule.
